A sundial computed for your latitude

The sundial that keeps clock time

Every ordinary sundial runs up to sixteen minutes fast or slow over the year. The Earth's path is an ellipse and its axis is tilted, so the sun itself is a slightly unreliable clock. In 1892 a British general and in 1966 a watchmaker from the Black Forest found the same remedy: replace the thin rod with a shaped roller and let the edge of its shadow carry the correction. This page builds one for your window sill, balcony or garden, ready to print.

Chapter one

Why the sun runs early and late

A sundial measures the sun's hour angle, and the sun does not cross the sky at a steady pace. In January the Earth is closest to the sun and moves fastest along its orbit; in July it dawdles. On top of that the tilt of the axis stretches and squeezes the sun's daily progress along the celestial equator. Add both effects and you get the equation of time: the sun is up to 16.4 minutes early in early November and 14.2 minutes late in mid-February. Only four days a year does it agree with the clock.

Astronomers draw this as a figure of eight, the analemma. The one on the right is computed for your location and year from the same formulas that shape the roller.

Chapter two

Bernhardt's roller

John Ryder Oliver patented the idea in London in 1892. Martin Bernhardt, a watchmaker from Freudenstadt, worked it out again from memory during years of captivity after the war, and built the first one in 1966 for a Sky & Telescope competition. About one hundred and fifty of his dials stand in public places today.

The trick is simple to say and lovely to watch. A roller throws a broad shadow with two edges. You read the leading edge only. As the seasons carry the sun higher and lower, a different height of the roller does the shadow casting, and at every height the roller is exactly as thick as that day's correction demands. The correction is not written on the dial; it is carved into the gnomon.

Every declination of the sun occurs twice a year, with different corrections. So there are two rollers, one for the rising half of the year and one for the falling half, swapped at the solstices.

Chapter three

A shape the light draws

The crescent is not styling. The plate is a negotiation with the low winter sun. Where the sun must reach the scale from above, the plate dips into a dish. Where the early and late sun must pass across to the far side, the wing thins into a blade in the plane of the scale, so that only for a few days around the equinoxes does its shadow get in the way. Wherever neither is possible the plate is cut away, and that cut is the inner edge of the crescent.

The generator traces every sunlit quarter hour of the winter half-years across the dial and lets those rays decide the shape. Change the latitude and the dish deepens, the wings twist, the horns reach for the summer hours.

    Chapter five

    Set it up

    Print the dial scale side up with tree supports under the wings, the rollers pin down with a brim, and the stand base down. Then, in this order:

    Level the base

    A bubble level on the disc, shims under the low edge. A tilt of one degree is a reading error of four minutes at noon.

    Aim the fin at true north

    Not magnetic north: correct your compass by the local declination, or use the shadow of a plumb line at local solar noon.

    Seat the dial

    The pin has a flat, the hub a matching socket. It only goes on one way, with the noon mark facing the pole.

    Choose the roller

    One groove from 21 December to 21 June, two grooves for the rest of the year. Read at the leading edge of the shadow, add an hour in summer time.

    Chapter six

    Where it is honest

    Near the solstices. For about three weeks in December and two in June the correction changes while the sun's height stands still, and no roller can be tangent to every ray. The error is shared between neighbouring days and stays within about two and a half minutes in winter, one in summer. Every other day reads to well within a minute. Bernhardt's own dials have the same limit.

    Near the equinoxes. When the sun skims the plane of the scale, the far wing tip shadows the first and last hour of the day for some weeks. The wings could avoid this with a sharp step, but the surface is kept continuous instead, which prints and looks better. The generator lists the exact dates for your dial.
